# How Insulin Resistance Drives PCOS

Insulin resistance is present in a majority of people with PCOS — estimates
commonly range from 65% to 80%, and it occurs in both lean and higher-weight
individuals. It is not a consequence of body weight alone.

The mechanism is worth understanding because it explains why lifestyle changes
work. When cells respond poorly to insulin, the pancreas compensates by
producing more of it. Persistently high circulating insulin does two things in
the ovary: it stimulates the theca cells to produce more androgens, and it
suppresses the liver's production of sex hormone binding globulin (SHBG). SHBG
is the protein that binds testosterone and keeps it inactive. Less SHBG means
more *free* testosterone even when total testosterone looks normal on a lab
report.

Elevated androgens then interfere with the follicle maturation that ovulation
depends on. Follicles begin to develop and stall, which is the appearance seen
on ultrasound. No ovulation means no progesterone, which means the cycle does
not complete normally.

This is a self-reinforcing loop, and it is also where it can be interrupted.
Anything that improves insulin sensitivity lowers circulating insulin, which
raises SHBG, which lowers free testosterone, which allows follicles to mature.

# What Actually Improves Insulin Sensitivity

The interventions with the strongest evidence are unglamorous and consistent
across the literature:

**Resistance training** improves insulin sensitivity through a mechanism
independent of weight loss. Skeletal muscle is the body's largest glucose sink,
and building muscle increases the capacity to clear glucose from the blood.
Two to three sessions a week is the commonly cited target.

**Any regular aerobic activity** improves glucose uptake for roughly 24 to 48
hours after a session. This is why frequency matters more than intensity: three
moderate 30-minute walks spread across a week do more for insulin sensitivity
than one exhausting weekend session.

**Modest weight reduction** where weight is elevated. Studies consistently find
that a 5% to 10% reduction in body weight is associated with improved cycle
regularity and ovulation — a much smaller change than most people assume is
necessary. The goal is metabolic improvement, not a particular number on a
scale.

**Sleep of adequate duration.** Restricting sleep to five hours a night for
even a week measurably reduces insulin sensitivity in healthy adults. Short
sleep also raises cortisol and increases appetite for high-carbohydrate food,
compounding the problem.

**Distributing carbohydrate intake** rather than eliminating it. Large single
loads of refined carbohydrate produce the largest insulin spikes. Pairing
carbohydrates with protein, fat and fibre slows gastric emptying and blunts
the glucose response.

# Signs Worth Noticing

Acanthosis nigricans — velvety darkened skin in the folds of the neck, armpits
or groin — is a visible marker of long-standing high insulin. Skin tags in the
same areas often accompany it. Strong cravings for sugar or refined starch,
particularly a mid-afternoon energy crash after a carbohydrate-heavy lunch, are
also common subjective signals.

None of these confirm insulin resistance on their own. A fasting insulin test,
a fasting glucose test, HbA1c, or an oral glucose tolerance test with paired
insulin measurements are how it is actually assessed. Current guidance
recommends that people with PCOS be screened for impaired glucose tolerance
periodically, since the risk of developing type 2 diabetes is meaningfully
elevated.
