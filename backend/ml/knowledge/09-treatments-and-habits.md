# Medical Treatments: An Educational Overview

Everything in this section is educational context so that a conversation with a
doctor is more productive. None of it is a recommendation, and none of these
medicines should be started, stopped or adjusted without a prescriber.

**Combined hormonal contraception** is commonly used as first-line therapy for
cycle regulation, hirsutism and acne when pregnancy is not being sought. It
provides the progestogen that protects the uterine lining and raises SHBG,
which lowers free androgen. Formulations differ in their androgenic profile,
and suitability depends on factors including migraine history, blood pressure,
smoking status and clotting risk.

**Cyclical or continuous progestogen** is an alternative for someone who cannot
or does not wish to use combined contraception, used to induce regular
withdrawal bleeds and protect the endometrium.

**Metformin** improves insulin sensitivity and is used for metabolic features,
and sometimes for cycle regularity. Gastrointestinal side effects are common at
the start and are usually reduced by beginning at a low dose, taking it with
food, and using the extended-release formulation. Long-term use can affect
vitamin B12 absorption, so periodic monitoring is usual.

**Letrozole** is generally recommended as first-line ovulation induction in
PCOS, having shown better live-birth rates than clomiphene citrate in major
trials.

**Spironolactone** is an anti-androgen used for hirsutism and hair loss. It
requires reliable contraception because of risk to a developing fetus, and
potassium is monitored.

**GLP-1 receptor agonists** are increasingly discussed for weight and metabolic
management in PCOS. Evidence specific to PCOS is still developing, access and
cost vary widely, and they are prescription medicines with a real side-effect
profile. This is a discussion to have with a doctor, not a decision to make
from social media.

**Inositol** is available without prescription and has the strongest evidence
among supplements, but should still be discussed with a clinician, especially
alongside metformin.

# Why Habits Matter More Than Plans

The interventions with the best evidence in PCOS are behavioural, and
behavioural interventions only work if they are sustained. The gap between
knowing what to do and doing it consistently is where most progress is lost.

Principles that hold up well in behaviour-change research:

**Start smaller than feels worthwhile.** A habit that feels trivially easy is
one you will still be doing in eight weeks. Two ten-minute walks beats a
planned hour that never happens. Consistency compounds; intensity does not.

**Attach a new habit to an existing one.** "After I make my morning coffee, I
do ten minutes of stretching" is far more reliable than "I will stretch more."
The existing routine supplies the cue.

**Make it obvious and easy.** Lay out the walking shoes. Keep cut vegetables at
eye level in the fridge. Reducing the friction of the desired action does more
than increasing motivation.

**Track it visibly.** Recording completion is itself motivating, and the record
is what turns a vague sense of "I've been doing okay" into evidence. It is also
what makes a clinical conversation concrete.

**Never miss twice.** One missed day is noise. Two missed days is the start of
a new pattern. The rule to protect is the return, not the streak.

**Expect the plateau.** Metabolic and hormonal changes take months. Cycle
regularity in particular is often reported to improve over three to six months
of consistent change, not weeks. Judging a programme after two weeks will
almost always produce a false negative.

**Choose one thing at a time.** Attempting to overhaul diet, exercise, sleep
and stress simultaneously reliably fails. Pick the weakest link, work on it for
a month, then add the next.

# What Progress Actually Looks Like

Weight is a poor short-term measure. It fluctuates daily with fluid, glycogen
and cycle phase, and it can stay flat while body composition and insulin
sensitivity genuinely improve.

Better signals to watch over a three-month horizon:

- Cycle length becoming more predictable, or periods returning
- Energy that is more stable through the afternoon
- Reduced intensity of sugar cravings
- Strength increasing — more repetitions or more load at the same effort
- Better sleep quality and fewer night wakings
- Waist measurement, which tracks visceral fat better than weight does
- Improvements in lab markers such as HbA1c, fasting insulin, SHBG and lipids

If none of these has moved after three genuinely consistent months, that is
useful information to bring to a clinician — it may indicate that a medical
intervention should be added rather than that you have failed at the
behavioural one.
