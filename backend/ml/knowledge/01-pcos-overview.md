# What PCOS Is

Polycystic ovary syndrome is a hormonal and metabolic condition that affects
how the ovaries work. It is one of the most common endocrine conditions in
women of reproductive age, with prevalence estimates generally ranging from
8% to 13% depending on which diagnostic criteria are applied and which
population is studied. A large share of cases — some studies suggest up to
70% — remain undiagnosed.

The name is misleading. The "cysts" seen on an ultrasound are not true cysts
but immature follicles that have stopped developing partway. Many people with
PCOS have no visible follicles at all, and many people with polycystic-appearing
ovaries do not have PCOS. Ovarian appearance alone never establishes the
diagnosis.

PCOS is best understood as three interacting problems: irregular or absent
ovulation, elevated androgen activity, and — in a majority of cases — insulin
resistance. These reinforce one another, which is why the condition tends to
persist without deliberate intervention.

# The Rotterdam Diagnostic Criteria

The most widely used framework requires **two of the following three**
features, after other conditions producing similar signs have been excluded:

1. **Irregular or absent ovulation.** In practice this usually presents as
   cycles longer than 35 days, fewer than eight periods a year, or periods
   that stop for months at a time.
2. **Clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism.** Clinical means visible signs:
   excess coarse hair on the face, chest or back (hirsutism), persistent acne,
   or scalp hair thinning in a male pattern. Biochemical means elevated
   testosterone or free androgen index on a blood test.
3. **Polycystic ovarian morphology on ultrasound.** Typically defined as 20 or
   more follicles measuring 2 to 9 mm in an ovary, or an increased ovarian
   volume.

Exclusion matters as much as inclusion. Thyroid disease, high prolactin,
non-classical congenital adrenal hyperplasia and Cushing's syndrome can all
mimic PCOS, and each has a different treatment. This is precisely why a
symptom questionnaire — including the one in this app — can never substitute
for a clinical assessment.

For adolescents within eight years of their first period, current guidance is
more cautious: irregular cycles are common and often normal in that window,
ultrasound findings are not reliable, and the recommendation is to re-evaluate
over time rather than label early.

# Why Diagnosis Is Often Delayed

Symptoms appear gradually and are frequently attributed to something else.
Irregular periods get blamed on stress, acne on adolescence, weight gain on
lifestyle, and fatigue on modern life. Each individual symptom has a mundane
explanation; it is the *combination* that is informative. Surveys of women with
PCOS repeatedly find that many saw three or more clinicians and waited over two
years before receiving a diagnosis.

Keeping a written record of cycle dates, symptoms and their timing is one of
the most useful things a person can do to shorten that path. Three months of
logged data converts a vague "my periods are a bit irregular" into a concrete
pattern a clinician can act on.
