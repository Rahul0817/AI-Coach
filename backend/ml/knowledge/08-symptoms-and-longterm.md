# The Symptom Picture

PCOS presents differently in different people, which is a large part of why it
is missed. Common features include:

- Irregular, infrequent or absent periods
- Difficulty conceiving
- Excess coarse hair on the face, chest, abdomen or back (hirsutism)
- Persistent acne, particularly along the jawline, that resists standard
  treatment
- Thinning hair at the crown or a widening part (androgenic alopecia)
- Weight gain, particularly around the abdomen, and difficulty losing it
- Darkened velvety skin in the neck, armpits or groin (acanthosis nigricans)
- Skin tags
- Fatigue, particularly after carbohydrate-heavy meals
- Strong cravings for sugar and refined starch
- Mood changes, anxiety and low mood
- Sleep disturbance

Presentation also varies by ethnicity. Hirsutism scores that are meaningful in
one population may not translate to another — for example, East Asian
populations tend to show less hirsutism at comparable androgen levels, while
South Asian populations tend to show insulin resistance at lower BMI
thresholds. Diagnostic thresholds that ignore this systematically under- and
over-diagnose different groups.

# Hair and Skin

**Hirsutism** results from androgens converting fine vellus hair to coarse
terminal hair in androgen-sensitive areas. It responds slowly to any
intervention because the hair growth cycle is long — expect to judge results
over six months, not six weeks. Options discussed clinically include combined
hormonal contraception, anti-androgens such as spironolactone (which requires
reliable contraception, as it can affect a developing fetus), topical
eflornithine, and mechanical or laser hair removal. Laser is most effective on
dark hair against lighter skin; newer wavelengths have improved outcomes for
darker skin tones.

**Androgenic hair loss** on the scalp is distressing and often dismissed. It is
distinct from telogen effluvium (diffuse shedding after a stressor, which
usually recovers). Early assessment by a dermatologist is worthwhile because
treatments work better before follicles are lost.

**Acne** in PCOS is typically hormonal in distribution — jawline, chin and neck
— and inflammatory rather than comedonal. It often responds poorly to
over-the-counter products aimed at teenage acne, and a dermatologist can offer
approaches targeted at the hormonal driver.

# Long-Term Health Risks

PCOS is a lifelong metabolic condition, not just a reproductive one, and the
associated risks are the reason management matters even for someone who is not
trying to conceive.

**Type 2 diabetes.** Risk is substantially elevated, and onset tends to occur
earlier than in the general population. Guidance recommends periodic glucose
screening — commonly every one to three years depending on other risk factors,
and more frequently during pregnancy.

**Gestational diabetes and pregnancy complications.** Rates of gestational
diabetes, pre-eclampsia and preterm birth are higher. Preconception assessment
and early glucose screening in pregnancy are recommended.

**Cardiovascular risk factors.** Dyslipidaemia, hypertension and metabolic
syndrome all occur at elevated rates. Blood pressure and lipids should be
checked periodically.

**Endometrial hyperplasia and cancer.** Risk is elevated where cycles are
chronically absent, because the uterine lining is exposed to oestrogen without
the opposing effect of progesterone. This risk is manageable — ensuring
regular withdrawal bleeds through medical means substantially addresses it,
which is why prolonged amenorrhoea should be discussed with a doctor rather
than tolerated.

**Obstructive sleep apnoea**, which occurs at elevated rates and further
worsens insulin resistance when untreated.

**Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease**, associated with the same insulin
resistance.

# The Constructive Framing

This list is long, and reading it can be frightening. Two things are worth
holding alongside it.

First, these are *elevated risks*, not certainties. Many people with PCOS never
develop any of them.

Second, the same small set of interventions — improving insulin sensitivity
through movement and diet, protecting sleep, managing stress, and maintaining
regular contact with a clinician — reduces essentially all of them
simultaneously. There is no separate protocol for each risk. That is an
unusually favourable position to be in with a chronic condition, and it is why
consistent everyday habits genuinely matter here rather than being generic
health advice.
