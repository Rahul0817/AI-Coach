# What a Typical Cycle Looks Like

A menstrual cycle is counted from the first day of full bleeding to the day
before the next period starts. In adults, cycles commonly run between 21 and 35
days, with bleeding lasting two to seven days. Variation of a few days between
cycles is normal.

The cycle has two halves separated by ovulation. In the follicular phase,
follicle-stimulating hormone drives a cohort of follicles to develop, one
becomes dominant, and rising oestrogen thickens the uterine lining. A surge of
luteinising hormone triggers ovulation. In the luteal phase, the collapsed
follicle becomes the corpus luteum and produces progesterone, which stabilises
the lining. If no pregnancy occurs, progesterone falls and the lining sheds.

The luteal phase is relatively fixed at roughly 12 to 14 days. Almost all
variation in total cycle length comes from the follicular phase — which is
exactly the phase disrupted in PCOS.

# Patterns That Warrant Attention

- **Oligomenorrhoea:** cycles longer than 35 days, or fewer than eight periods
  per year. This is the most common presentation in PCOS.
- **Amenorrhoea:** no period for three months or more in someone who was
  previously menstruating.
- **Highly variable length:** cycles swinging between, say, 24 and 50 days.
  Variance is informative in its own right; a consistently long 38-day cycle is
  a different signal from cycles that are unpredictable.
- **Very heavy or prolonged bleeding**, or bleeding between periods.

A single unusual cycle is common and rarely meaningful. Illness, travel, an
intense training block, significant stress or weight change can all delay
ovulation once. The pattern across three or more cycles is what matters.

Prolonged absence of periods deserves medical attention for a specific reason:
without ovulation there is no progesterone to oppose oestrogen, and the uterine
lining can build up unchecked over time. This is associated with an increased
risk of endometrial hyperplasia. Current guidance generally suggests that
people with PCOS should have a withdrawal bleed at least every three to four
months, which is achievable through several medical routes. This is a
conversation to have with a doctor, not something to manage alone.

# Why Ovulation Prediction Is Unreliable in PCOS

Calendar-based prediction assumes a consistent follicular phase. In PCOS it is
not consistent, so calendar predictions — including the one this app displays —
carry substantial uncertainty and should be treated as a rough guide only.

Ovulation predictor kits that detect the LH surge are also less reliable in
PCOS, because baseline LH is often chronically elevated, producing readings
that look like a surge when no ovulation follows.

More reliable indicators are basal body temperature tracking, which shows a
sustained rise *after* ovulation has already happened, and cervical mucus
changes. A mid-luteal progesterone blood test is the clinical method for
confirming that ovulation occurred.

**None of this app's cycle predictions should be used as contraception.**

# Fertility

PCOS is one of the most common causes of anovulatory infertility, and it is
also one of the most treatable. The important framing is that difficulty
conceiving in PCOS is usually a problem of *ovulation*, not of egg quality or
uterine receptivity — and ovulation can often be restored.

Approaches used clinically, in rough order of escalation:

1. **Lifestyle modification.** Where weight is elevated, a 5% to 10% reduction
   is associated with restored ovulation in a meaningful proportion of cases.
   Improving insulin sensitivity through resistance training and dietary change
   helps independently.
2. **Ovulation induction medication.** Letrozole is now generally recommended
   as first-line therapy in PCOS, having outperformed clomiphene citrate for
   live birth rates in major trials.
3. **Metformin**, sometimes used alongside, particularly where insulin
   resistance is prominent.
4. **Gonadotropins** or **laparoscopic ovarian drilling** in selected cases.
5. **IVF**, where other approaches have not succeeded. People with PCOS respond
   strongly to stimulation, so protocols are adjusted to manage the risk of
   ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome.

Ovarian reserve is often *higher* than average in PCOS — AMH levels are
typically elevated — so the common fear that PCOS means running out of eggs is
generally misplaced. AMH is a marker of follicle count, not of fertility.

Anyone under 35 who has been trying to conceive for 12 months, or over 35
trying for 6 months, should seek specialist assessment. With known irregular
cycles it is reasonable to seek advice earlier rather than waiting out the
full period.
