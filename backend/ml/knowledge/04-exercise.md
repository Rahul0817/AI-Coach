# Why Exercise Works Beyond Weight Loss

The most common misunderstanding about exercise and PCOS is that its value lies
in burning calories. It does not. Exercise improves insulin sensitivity through
mechanisms that operate whether or not body weight changes: contracting muscle
takes up glucose via GLUT4 transporters without requiring insulin, and training
increases the total amount of muscle available to do so.

Trials in PCOS populations have found improvements in insulin sensitivity,
androgen levels and cycle regularity in participants whose weight did not
change. This matters because weight-focused advice is discouraging for people
whose weight is stubborn — which, given the metabolic picture, is many of them.

# The Core Prescription

General physical-activity guidance recommends at least 150 minutes of moderate
activity per week, or 75 minutes of vigorous activity, plus muscle-strengthening
work on two or more days. For PCOS specifically, the strength component
deserves more emphasis than it usually gets.

**Resistance training, 2 to 3 sessions per week.** This is the highest-value
component. Compound movements that recruit large muscle groups — squats,
hip hinges, presses, rows, carries — build the glucose-disposal capacity that
matters. Progressive overload is the mechanism: the load, repetitions or sets
must increase over time or adaptation stops.

**Moderate aerobic activity, most days.** Brisk walking counts. So does
cycling, swimming or dancing. The frequency is what matters, because the
insulin-sensitising effect of a session lasts roughly 24 to 48 hours.

**Post-meal movement.** Ten minutes of walking after the largest meal of the
day is one of the highest-return habits available, and it does not require
scheduling a workout.

# On High-Intensity Interval Training

HIIT is time-efficient and produces genuine improvements in cardiorespiratory
fitness and insulin sensitivity. It is not, however, universally appropriate.

For people with PCOS who report high stress, poor sleep or persistent fatigue,
frequent high-intensity work can be counterproductive. Intense exercise raises
cortisol acutely; in someone already under sustained stress with inadequate
recovery, stacking more of it can worsen sleep and leave them feeling worse
rather than better.

A reasonable approach is one or two HIIT sessions a week at most, placed on
days with adequate sleep behind them, with the remainder of the week built from
strength work and moderate aerobic activity.

# Starting From Sedentary

The failure mode for someone starting from nothing is doing too much in week
one and stopping in week three. A conservative progression:

- **Weeks 1 to 2:** a 15-minute walk daily, plus two 15-minute bodyweight
  sessions (squats to a chair, wall or knee push-ups, glute bridges, planks).
- **Weeks 3 to 4:** extend walks to 25 minutes; add load or repetitions to the
  strength sessions.
- **Weeks 5 to 8:** two to three 35-minute strength sessions with progressive
  load; walks on the remaining days.
- **Beyond:** introduce one interval session per week if recovery is good.

Consistency beats intensity by a wide margin over a six-month horizon. Two
sessions a week completed for six months outperform five sessions a week
abandoned after three.

# Training Around the Cycle

Some people find strength output is higher in the follicular phase (from the
start of a period to ovulation) and that the luteal phase brings more fatigue
and a higher perceived effort at the same load. The evidence for structured
cycle-based periodisation is still limited and inconsistent, so treat this as
something to observe in your own logs rather than a rule to follow.

For people with irregular cycles — most people with PCOS — cycle-phase
programming is impractical anyway. Training according to how recovered you feel
is the more useful heuristic.

# When to Be Careful

Stop and seek medical advice for chest pain, unusual shortness of breath,
dizziness or fainting during exercise. If you have a joint condition, are
pregnant, or have been sedentary for a long period with other health
conditions, get clearance from a doctor before starting a vigorous programme.
