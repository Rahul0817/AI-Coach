# There Is No Single "PCOS Diet"

The evidence does not support one specific diet over all others. Mediterranean,
lower-carbohydrate, low-glycaemic-index and simply calorie-controlled patterns
have all shown benefit in trials. What they share is more informative than what
separates them: reduced refined carbohydrate, adequate protein, high fibre, and
sustainability over months rather than weeks.

The best diet for PCOS is the nutritionally adequate one a person can actually
maintain. Restrictive plans that eliminate entire food groups tend to be
abandoned, and repeated cycles of restriction and abandonment are associated
with disordered eating — a risk that is already elevated in this population.

# Glycaemic Load, Not Just Glycaemic Index

Glycaemic index measures how quickly a carbohydrate raises blood glucose.
Glycaemic *load* also accounts for how much of it you eat, which is the number
that matters in practice. Watermelon has a high GI but a low glycaemic load in
a normal portion.

Practical rules that lower glycaemic load without counting anything:

- **Never eat a naked carbohydrate.** Always pair bread, rice, pasta, potato or
  fruit with a protein or fat source. Toast with eggs behaves very differently
  from toast alone.
- **Prefer intact grains to flour.** Whole oats beat instant oats; whole wheat
  berries beat whole-wheat flour. Grinding a grain raises its glycaemic
  response even when the fibre content is identical.
- **Cool your starches.** Cooking and then cooling rice or potatoes forms
  resistant starch, which is digested more slowly. Reheating retains much of
  the effect.
- **Front-load fibre.** Eating vegetables or salad at the start of a meal
  measurably reduces the glucose peak from the carbohydrate that follows.
- **Move after eating.** A ten-minute walk after a meal reduces the post-meal
  glucose rise, because contracting muscle takes up glucose without needing
  insulin.

# Protein and Fibre Targets

Protein increases satiety, preserves lean mass during weight loss, and has a
minimal effect on insulin relative to carbohydrate. A commonly used target is
roughly 1.2 to 1.6 grams per kilogram of body weight per day, distributed
across meals rather than concentrated at dinner. Around 25 to 30 grams at
breakfast is particularly useful, because a protein-rich breakfast reliably
reduces cravings later in the day.

Fibre intake in most populations sits well below the recommended 25 to 30 grams
a day. Fibre slows glucose absorption, feeds gut bacteria that produce
short-chain fatty acids, and supports the excretion of excess oestrogen through
the digestive tract. Legumes, whole grains, vegetables, nuts, seeds and berries
are the practical sources.

# Anti-Inflammatory Emphasis

PCOS is associated with low-grade chronic inflammation. Dietary patterns that
reduce inflammatory markers emphasise oily fish, olive oil, nuts, seeds,
colourful vegetables and herbs and spices, while limiting ultra-processed food,
industrially refined seed oils used at high heat, and added sugar.

Omega-3 fatty acids from oily fish two to three times a week are the most
consistently supported single addition.

# Adapting to South Asian and Other Rice-Based Cuisines

Advice built around Western meal structures often fails in practice. Useful
adaptations that preserve the cuisine:

- Replace part of the rice portion with dal, rajma or chana. The legume adds
  protein and fibre and lowers the meal's glycaemic load without removing rice.
- Choose hand-pounded or parboiled rice, or millets such as ragi, bajra and
  jowar, which have a lower glycaemic response than polished white rice.
- Add a bowl of curd or a paneer or egg portion to any roti-and-sabzi meal to
  raise its protein content.
- Use besan (gram flour) or mixed-millet flour for a proportion of chapati
  flour to increase protein and fibre.
- Keep vegetables at roughly half the plate; the traditional sabzi portion is
  usually smaller than that.

# Supplements: What the Evidence Actually Says

**Inositol** (usually myo-inositol, sometimes combined with D-chiro-inositol in
a 40:1 ratio) has the most supportive evidence of the commonly used
supplements, with trials reporting improvements in insulin sensitivity and
ovulation. Effect sizes vary considerably between studies.

**Vitamin D** deficiency is common in PCOS, and correcting a documented
deficiency is reasonable. Supplementing without a deficiency has not shown
consistent benefit.

**Omega-3**, **magnesium**, **berberine**, **NAC** and **chromium** all have
some supporting studies, generally smaller and of lower quality.

Supplements are not regulated as medicines, doses in trials often exceed what
retail products contain, and some interact with prescription drugs — berberine
in particular can compound the effect of glucose-lowering medication. Discuss
any supplement with a doctor or pharmacist before starting it, especially if
you take metformin, hormonal contraception or thyroid medication.
